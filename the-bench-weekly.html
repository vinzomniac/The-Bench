Trend Radar

Skincare active — regeneration

PDRN (polydeoxyribonucleotide)

Hype — thin evidenceSignal 92 / 100

PDRN topped the Spate × NYSCC 2026 report, driven by K-beauty and injectable-to-topical crossover. It is touted for tissue repair, collagen production and inflammation reduction via adenosine receptor activation. Demand is now mainstream, but topical cosmetic evidence lags the injectable and wound literature.

Movement — +322.3% YoY growth; 1.1 million increase in Spate popularity, popularity index 1.4 million

Evidence note: Spate measures search and social demand, not efficacy. No robust topical randomised trial is cited in the excerpt, and a large DNA polymer raises penetration questions, so repair and collagen claims need supplier topical clinicals before use.

Source: www.happi.com

Skincare active — barrier / soothing

Centella asiatica

Solid signalSignal 78 / 100

Centella remains a barrier and soothing staple, now repositioned for healthy ageing and post-procedure care. Standardised madecassoside and asiaticoside fractions give formulators a defensible active, not just a marketing extract. Momentum is demand-led, but the ingredient itself is not new.

Movement — Ranked in the top 3 fastest-rising actives in the Spate × NYSCC 2026 Ingredient Trends Report, alongside PDRN and beta-glucan

Evidence note: Established soothing and barrier use with standardised actives; differentiate by extract specification and dose, not by the centella name alone.

Source: www.skinstacker.com

Skincare active — hydration / barrier

Beta-glucan

Solid signalSignal 74 / 100

Beta-glucan is rising as a hydrating, soothing barrier active, valued for immediate skin feel and tolerance on sensitised skin. It fits the regeneration, barrier and hydration cluster dominating 2026 briefs. Unlike PDRN, its mechanism and tolerability are well understood at cosmetic doses.

Movement — Ranked in the top 3 fastest-rising actives in the Spate × NYSCC 2026 Ingredient Trends Report, alongside PDRN and centella asiatica

Evidence note: Hydration and soothing support is credible, but film-forming hydration should not be conflated with deep repair; dose and molecular weight and source — oat, yeast or mushroom — matter.

Source: www.skinstacker.com

Solid signalSignal 68 / 100

Ingredient Spotlights

Use level · 0.1–1.0%

PDRN

INCI: Sodium DNA

What it does: Regenerative and soothing active; polydeoxyribonucleotide fragments claimed to support tissue repair and reduce inflammation via adenosine A2A receptor signalling.

Why now: Topped Spate × NYSCC 2026 and grew 322.3% YoY, so clients will ask for it. Formulate it as a soothing and hydration story unless the supplier supplies topical clinicals for repair claims.

Compatibility & handling

Water-soluble, polyanionic. Add in cool-down below 40°C at pH 5.0–7.0; avoid prolonged high heat, strong oxidisers and high levels of cationic polymers that can complex DNA. Salmon/trout-derived — check vegan, allergen, halal/kosher and sourcing claims before use.

Source: www.happi.com

Use level · 0.5–2.0%

Ignilyte

INCI: Hylocereus Undatus Stem Cell Extract

What it does: Bioelectric and regenerative active from dragon-fruit stem cells, positioned to reawaken skin bioelectrical signalling for deep regeneration and vitality.

Why now: Gold, Innovation Zone Best Ingredient — Actives, in-cosmetics Asia 2026. It gives a differentiated regeneration story beyond PDRN, but demand the supplier's in-vivo data before using bioelectric claims.

Compatibility & handling

Water-soluble plant-cell active; add in cool-down below 40°C, pH 4.5–7.0. Compatible with typical humectant and emulsion systems; avoid high heat, high ethanol and strong oxidisers, and verify preservative compatibility.

Source: www.personalcaremagazine.com

Use level · 0.5–2.0%

Glycare SK

INCI: 2'-Fucosyllactose

What it does: Human milk oligosaccharide (HMO) active translated to skincare; positioned for skin microbiome balance, barrier support and soothing.

Why now: Silver, Innovation Zone Best Ingredient — Actives, in-cosmetics Asia 2026. HMOs are a credible new microbiome angle, but check the exact HMO, dose and clinical endpoint — microbiome claims invite regulatory scrutiny.

Compatibility & handling

Water-soluble oligosaccharide; add to the water phase, generally tolerant of typical skincare heat and pH ranges. Compatible with most emulsions and serums; it is not a preservative or humectant replacement, so do not rely on it for preservation or hydration.

Source: www.personalcaremagazine.com

Use level · 2.0–10.0%

Dowsil EL-9400 Hybrid Elastomer Blend

INCI: Dimethicone (and) Dimethicone/Vinyl Dimethicone Crosspolymer

What it does: Silicone elastomer blend for soft-focus, mattifying and long-wear performance; improves spread, powdery skin feel and film uniformity in colour and skincare.

Why now: Silver, Innovation Zone Best Ingredient — Functional, in-cosmetics Asia 2026. Useful where briefs demand long-wear comfort without a heavy wax load, but it is a sensorial and film benefit — not an active claim.

Compatibility & handling

Oil and silicone-phase only, not water-soluble. Disperse in silicones, esters and oils; compatible with waxes and pigments in anhydrous or W/S systems. Do not expect stability in a simple O/W system without a suitable emulsifier, and test for pilling with high levels of film-formers.

Source: www.happi.com

Regulatory Watch

The regulatory moves that change what you can formulate, label, and ship — with what they mean at the bench.

United States — FDA / MoCRA

MoCRA: FDA facility registration, product listing and adverse-event reporting are now mandatory

MoCRA changed US cosmetics regulation — since December 2022, FDA facility registration, product listing and adverse-event reporting are now mandatory. The US still does not require pre-market safety assessment, unlike the EU, so the burden shifts to the brand's own substantiation and record-keeping.

At your bench: No launch without a registered facility and listed product; formulators and consultants must build listing, adverse-event capture and safety files into the development timeline, not after stability.

Source: www.clearship.ai

European Union

EU: CPSR by a qualified assessor is required before any sale

The EU is the strictest market in scope: a Cosmetic Product Safety Report (CPSR) by a qualified assessor is required before a single unit can be sold. The US and Canada do not require pre-market safety assessments, so EU-bound formulas cannot be treated as US-ready.

At your bench: Formulators must lock formula, specifications, stability and toxicological data for assessor review well before launch; late changes trigger reassessment and delay.

Source: www.clearship.ai

European Union / United Kingdom

EU and UK: separate registrations, and an EU Responsible Person is non-negotiable

The US (FDA/MoCRA), EU (CPNP) and UK (SCPN) each require separate registrations — one notification does not cover all three. Selling in the EU without an appointed EU Responsible Person is illegal, regardless of where the brand is based.

At your bench: Export briefs need three parallel notification tracks and an EU Responsible Person appointed before launch; formulators must supply the full dossier early enough for the Responsible Person and CPNP process.

Source: veilta.com

Copper Tripeptide-1 (GHK-Cu): The Blue Peptide That Tells You When It’s Dying

Most actives fail quietly. You find out at the 12-week stability pull, or worse, you never find out at all — the formula ships, the active is dead on arrival, and nobody can tell from looking at the jar.

GHK-Cu is not one of those actives. It’s a 1:1 complex of copper(II) and the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, and the copper complex is the blue color. Not a dye, not a marketing tint — the color is the molecule holding its copper. When the blue fades, shifts green, or disappears, the complex has come apart and what you’re selling is an expensive peptide solution with free copper floating in it. It is the only active I work with that runs its own visible assay, every day, on the shelf. This entry is about keeping it alive.

What it actually is

GHK was first isolated from human plasma in 1973 (Loren Pickart’s work), and it circulates in us naturally — roughly 200 ng/mL in plasma at age 20, falling to about 80 ng/mL by age 60. It’s both a signal peptide and a carrier: the histidine and N-terminal nitrogens grip Cu²⁺ and hand it to the skin, where copper is a required cofactor for lysyl oxidase — the enzyme that cross-links collagen and elastin — and for superoxide dismutase. The published evidence is mostly in vitro work plus small 12-week topical studies reporting firmer, denser-looking skin and improved fine lines and tone. That’s enough to formulate with, not enough to make drug claims. Keep finished-product language to appearance claims.

Two identity traps before you order. Free GHK (INCI: Tripeptide-1) has no copper and no color — different material. Bis(Tripeptide-1) Copper Acetate is a 2:1 complex — also different. Check the salt form and assay on the COA of whatever you buy.

The working window

  • Dose: pure powder typically 0.05–0.5% (literature runs to 1.0%). Start a face serum at 0.1–0.2%; the blue gets strong toward the top of the range. Most suppliers sell pre-diluted solutions instead — always back-calculate to pure peptide before comparing prices or doses, because a “2% copper peptide serum” usually means 2% of a 1% solution, i.e. 0.02% actual.
  • pH: formulate 5.0–7.0, work at 5.5–6.5, finish near 5.8.
  • Phase and temperature: water phase only, added last, in cool-down, below 40°C. Pre-dissolve the powder in 5–10% reserved batch water to a uniform blue premix and stir in gently. Held above ~45°C, the copper dissociates.
  • Water: purified. Competing metal ions in hard water are one more thing pulling at the complex.

What strips the copper

The chelate is strong, but it is not armor. The kill list, in rough order of how often I see each in real formulas:

  1. Strong chelators. EDTA in either salt form, high levels of GLDA, sodium phytate. These are in half the “clean” formulas on the market as insurance ingredients, added by habit. In a GHK-Cu formula, build chelator-free on purpose and say so in your batch notes.
  2. Low pH. Below about pH 4.5–5.0 the complex unzips: copper dissociates, color fades, activity goes with it — and free copper is a pro-oxidant, so a dead copper peptide is worse than no copper peptide. This is why GHK-Cu never shares a bottle with a real vitamin C serum or a direct-acid peel. Separate steps, separate times of day.
  3. Reducing agents. L-ascorbic acid again (it’s a pH problem and a redox problem), glutathione and other thiols, sulfites.
  4. Heat and time. Slow dissociation is still dissociation. Accelerated stability with color retention and pH as the readouts is not optional on this ingredient — it is the stability program.

The gluconolactone question, from my own bench

This is the one I had to work out live, on a peptide serum carrying 0.3% GHK-Cu powder: the formula also carried a gluconolactone preservative-booster blend at 0.75%, and the active complex itself contained more gluconolactone. Is gluconolactone a chelator problem for GHK-Cu?

The careful answer is: a modest one, through two different doors. Published analytical work (an HPLC-MS study in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology) shows gluconolactone can complex Cu(II) in a 2:1 ratio. GHK’s grip on copper is far stronger than gluconate’s, so at equal footing the peptide wins — but gluconolactone is typically present in large molar excess, and complexes are equilibria, not padlocks. The bigger door is slower: gluconolactone hydrolyzes to gluconic acid over time, and an unbuffered system drifts down toward pH 3–4. Drift below pH 5 unzips the complex regardless of who wins the binding contest.

My working rules from that batch: gluconolactone at 0.5% or under as a booster is a comfort zone, not a red line; let it equilibrate fully in the water phase (give it an hour) before you set pH; finish at 5.5–6.0; peptide goes in last, in cool-down; and let the color in accelerated stability deliver the verdict. There is no published head-to-head study of gluconolactone against GHK-Cu — anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing with more confidence than the data has.

The pH adjuster matters more than you think

When I needed to bring that same batch up to pH 5.5–6, the reflex move — a sodium citrate buffer — was the wrong one here. Citrate is a genuine multidentate chelator of Cu²⁺ (its third pKa sits around 6.4, right at working pH), so buffering a copper peptide with citrate is protecting the pH with one hand and pulling at the copper with the other. Sodium hydroxide is the cleaner call: it adjusts without chelating. It doesn’t buffer, so the process has to do the buffering’s job — equilibrate first, dose the NaOH as a dilute 10–20% solution so you don’t overshoot locally, and re-check pH at 24 hours and again at one week, after the peptide and everything else is in. A pH you set before the peptides go in is a draft, not a result.

Watch-outs that don’t show up on the spec sheet

  • Oxidation-prone neighbors. A high load of polyunsaturated oils (rosehip is the usual suspect) sits one dissociation event away from free copper, and free copper catalyzes exactly the oxidation you’re worried about. If you must run PUFA oils beside GHK-Cu, dose a deliberate antioxidant (tocopherol around 0.2–0.5%) and watch both the color and the odor in stability.
  • Color math. Blue complex plus carotenoid-gold oils can push an emulsion greenish-teal. Nobody’s data sheet warns you about the aesthetics. Mock it up before you fall in love with the formula.
  • Preservation is a separate fight. A peptide-and-ferment serum is a nutrient-rich water phase, and gentle preservative systems sized for a simple gel may not hold it. None of the above matters if the challenge test fails — that test is still the verdict.
  • Packaging. Opaque, airless, cool and dark. A dropper bottle is a contamination route reopened at every use; on a fragile, lightly preserved peptide formula, argue for the pump.

The short version

Build the base chelator-free. Set your pH after everything acidic has equilibrated, with dilute NaOH, not citrate. Pre-dissolve the peptide, add it last and cool, and re-check pH tomorrow. Then let the blue do its job: if the color holds through accelerated stability, the complex held. If it fades, something in the formula took the copper — and now you know the short list of suspects to interrogate first.
